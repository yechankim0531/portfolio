Sports has always been more than entertainment. The market for it has expanded significantly in recent years, and the numbers reflect that. Shohei Ohtani recently signed a 10-year, $700 million contract. Scottie Scheffler is earning at rates Tiger Woods never approached at his peak. Tournament prize money in golf has nearly doubled in just a few years. The question worth asking is not just why this is happening, but what it means for society going forward.

The growth is being driven by a surge of institutional investment into teams and leagues, global streaming rights, and the monetization of fan engagement. But to understand where sports is headed, it helps to look at where it came from.

Organized sports were designed from the beginning to unite communities and absorb attention. The Romans understood this clearly. Gladiatorial games were not just entertainment — they were a deliberate tool for managing the masses, channeling energy that might otherwise turn toward political frustration into something controlled and consumable. Modern sports leagues reflect the same logic. They are owned by wealthy investors, structured around artificial competitions, and serve as a shared focal point for people who have little else in common. I say this as a college football fan who has not missed a Georgia Bulldogs game since 2017. I am not above it.

This connects to a bigger question about what comes next. With AI advancing rapidly and the possibility of AGI on the horizon, humanity may eventually reach a point where far less human labor is required. That means more leisure time, and more leisure time means more demand for entertainment. Sports and media could expand dramatically in that scenario.

But more free time does not mean a more equal world. The wealth gap will likely widen alongside the intelligence gap. Advanced technology will become harder for the average person to understand, and the model of the world that elites hold will be far more sophisticated than what most people can access. Sports fills that gap in a way almost nothing else can. Even if someone does not understand how the economy works or how AI is reshaping labor markets, they can fully understand a baseball game. They can know the statistics, follow the drama, and feel genuine mastery over something. That is not trivial. It satisfies a human need to comprehend and belong, and without that outlet, the frustration of feeling left behind could turn into something more disruptive.

Sports also provides something rare in modern life: a structure where outcomes are predictable within clear rules, where hope is always present, and where victory — even experienced secondhand — feels real. For people who are not winning in their own lives, following a team offers a form of shared triumph that is otherwise hard to come by. It also holds out the possibility, however slim, of rising from nothing to extreme wealth and fame through athletic talent. This mirrors capitalism's own promise of upward mobility, and it serves a similar function: it keeps people striving and invested in the system.

The ownership structure of sports is also shifting. Right now a small number of global leagues dominate consistent viewership, functioning as monopolies within their sports. As more capital flows in, that will change. LIV Golf is an early example — a well-funded challenger that directly disrupted the PGA Tour's century-old model. It was controversial, but it forced change and pushed athlete compensation higher. We will see more of this across other sports. When leagues compete for athletes the way companies compete for talent, salaries rise and the product improves.

The long arc here points toward sports becoming a larger and more central institution in public life, not a smaller one. As the complexity of the real world outpaces what most people can engage with, the appeal of something structured, fair, and fully comprehensible will only grow. Sports will not save society from its contradictions. But it may be one of the few things that holds it together while those contradictions play out.
